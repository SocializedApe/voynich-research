## 2. Where the hypotheses stand (judgement*)

**Overall judgement (29 Oct 2026, after #41-#45; cryptocypher-4a session).** The leading account is meaningless text
made page by page by several makers from one shared, rigid word template. For it: no calibrated cipher search finds
anything (#41-#45 here; Zhang & Sheng, arXiv 2610.34499, across 16 languages and most cipher families); the scribes
differ as much as independent gibberish writers and twice as much as real scribes copying a real text (#20, #22); a
redrawn plant brings no shared words to its second page, labels do not recur in the text, herbal entries share no
formula and zodiac labels carry no positional names (#11, #16, #17). Against a simple 'random gibberish' reading: the
words follow a template that free invention does not produce (#5, #8, #23), and leaf vocabulary is as local as in a
real topical text (#15), so the makers worked inside a learned system with page-level material. The one cipher still
standing in the literature, a second-key nomenclator with more than half of the words miscopied, would leave the text
unreadable in any case. Not decided: the physical and historical questions (who, where, from which models, why).

What no tested model supplies: the change of glyph statistics between parts of the book (A/B drift, 0.050 within
sections against 0.004-0.007 for a real topical text). Leaf and page locality, by contrast, is what a real topical
text gives (#15). The text is local but not tied to its pictures (#16, #17): a plant redrawn elsewhere brings no
shared words, herbal entries share no formula.

What the data now ask of any explanation: template-built words (#5, #8), page-specific vocabulary that holds across
the whole book (#7), language-like spread of words over pages (#8), and a word sequence that is less predictable than
syntax but more repetitive and more clustered than any language (#9).

Limit on meaning tests: under Naibbe-type letter ciphers at Voynich's length, meaningful and scrambled plaintext give
the same word-order statistics (#25). Word-sequence tests, n-gram or neural (#10), cannot show meaning under them.

What the scribe tests now fix (#22, #26-#28): one shared word grammar used by all scribes, with personal habits
larger than real scribes' dialect spelling. Several systems (ciphers per scribe or per Currier group) and one system
used alike by all are both excluded; Currier A/B is a difference of convention within one system, following the
language more than the hand (#30). The duel (#33) leaves a trade-off: the tested cipher keeps the word statistics
(b3) but cannot carry the A/B and page conventions; the improviser carries them only by breaking the word statistics.
What is missing is a mechanism that changes a convention between A and B and from quire to quire (#32) while keeping
Voynich's word statistics. A slot-wise convention layer (#35, #36) is the right kind of piece (it improves b3, habits and
drift on both bases) but leaves document- and quire-scale variation short, and it does not separate cipher from
improvised text.
